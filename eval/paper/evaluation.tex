\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Evaluation Objectives and Scope}
\label{sec:objectives}
Our evaluation addresses whether declarative specifications of marketplace
validation semantics can be enforced on the commit path of a permissioned
ledger at a cost that is small relative to consensus, while remaining
authoritative when the blockchain state those specifications depend upon
changes. We specifically evaluate trade-offs between (1) imperative
validation, where ownership, advertisement-status, and offer-linkage rules
are embedded in engine code, and (2) declarative SHACL constraints, where
the same rules are explicit, inspectable, and amenable to dependency-aware
re-validation.

\textbf{Evaluation criteria.} Given the inherent declarative overhead (RDF
materialization, HTTP invocation of the constraint engine, and graph
evaluation), we consider parity with the imperative Direct baseline within
the consensus-dominated latency envelope a success, as the value proposition
extends beyond validation speed to include: (1) inspectability and
portability of the constraints themselves, (2) dependency-aware
re-validation in which cached verdicts are reused only while the referenced
state is unchanged, and (3) fail-closed rejection of semantically invalid
transactions. We compare Direct, SHACL, and SHACL+$\Delta$ within
SmartChainDB (BigchainDB on Tendermint) to isolate the impact of the
validation paradigm from consensus differences, and we port the same shapes
and treatments to Hyperledger Fabric~2.5 to assess whether the observed
behavior is specific to one engine.

The focus of our evaluation is not overall system throughput, as ledger
throughput depends on consensus protocol, block parameters, network
topology, and storage backend, which are outside our scope. Rather, we aim
to understand the impact of the validation paradigm on per-transaction
cost, on the commit-path latency envelope, and on correctness under state
change. We therefore report within-engine comparisons and interpret the
Fabric panel as a portability reference rather than as a cross-platform
ranking.

Our evaluation addresses four research questions:

\textbf{RQ1: Declarative overhead and differential re-validation.} What is
the per-transaction validation cost of SHACL relative to Direct, and under
what conditions does SHACL+$\Delta$ reuse a cached verdict rather than
re-executing constraint evaluation?

\textbf{RQ2: End-to-end impact.} How do the three approaches compare in
committed lifecycle latency, in the commit-latency distribution of
contended offers, and in achieved throughput under closed-loop load, and
what fraction of end-to-end time is attributable to validation?

\textbf{RQ3: Fail-closed correctness.} Do SHACL and SHACL+$\Delta$ reject
semantically invalid transactions (advertisements by a non-owner, offers
against a closed advertisement) with the same outcomes as Direct, and does
verdict caching preserve those outcomes when the referenced state changes?

\textbf{RQ4: Portability.} Do the same shapes and treatments exhibit the
same qualitative behavior on Hyperledger Fabric, whose execute--order--validate
pipeline differs from Tendermint's order--execute model?

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsubsection{Hardware and Environment}
All experiments were conducted on a single workstation running WSL2 with
Docker Engine~27.5.1. Latency and load experiments use one SmartChainDB
validator (Tendermint v0.31.5, MongoDB~3.6, one gunicorn worker) together
with the SHACL constraint engine as a sidecar container. Fail-closed
experiments were re-run on a four-validator LAN deployment (four Tendermint
voting keys, four MongoDB/SHACL/BigchainDB replicas, full mesh with
$n_{\mathrm{peers}}{=}3$). The Fabric deployment is the 2.5.12 test-network
with two endorsing peers (one per organization), a single orderer, and Go
marketplace chaincode. Table~\ref{tab:setup} summarizes the configurations.

\subsubsection{Implementations}
We evaluate three implementations of identical marketplace transaction
semantics on each engine.

\textbf{Direct:} Imperative validation integrated in the engine
(server-side Python methods in SmartChainDB; Go chaincode with MVCC
version checks on Fabric). Validation logic is implemented procedurally
within the platform rather than as declarative constraints. This serves as
the baseline against which declarative overhead is measured.

\textbf{SHACL:} Declarative validation without optimization. Each
transaction is converted to RDF, merged with the referenced blockchain state
(asset ownership, effective advertisement status, offer linkage), and
validated against the shape repository over HTTP. Validation results are not
cached; every validation phase re-executes constraint evaluation. The engine
is fail-closed: a constraint violation or an unreachable engine rejects the
transaction.

\textbf{SHACL+$\Delta$:} SHACL validation with differential re-validation.
Verdicts are cached under the key $\langle$tx-id, dependency-state
hash$\rangle$, where the hash is computed over the \emph{values} of the
referenced entities (current owner, effective advertisement status, offer
state). A cached verdict is reused only if the recomputed hash matches;
committed transactions invalidate reverse-indexed entries; and a conflict
tracker may abort an in-flight validation if a dependency generation
advances during the HTTP round-trip.

All three treatments share the same client, the same transaction types, and
the same state transitions on a given engine, differing only in how
validation is specified and executed. On Fabric, SHACL and SHACL+$\Delta$
are evaluated in the client before endorsement, and the $\Delta$ variant
issues a second SHACL call against the same facts to emulate the
re-validation that Tendermint performs natively in CheckTx.

\subsubsection{Workload Design}
\textbf{Goals.} (1) Exercise the end-to-end dependency structure among
transaction types (owner $\to$ advertisement $\to$ offer $\to$ sale $\to$
return); (2) control the position at which referenced state changes so that
cache reuse, invalidation, and rejection can be attributed to specific
operations; (3) support both validator-only timing and committed
end-to-end timing; (4) support closed-loop load at several concurrency
levels.

\textbf{Transaction universe.} The framework supports seven marketplace
transaction types (Create, Advertise, UpdateAdv, BuyOffer, Sell,
RequestReturn, AcceptReturn). The full lifecycle experiment exercises all
seven; the contention mixes and the load experiment use the Create,
Advertise, UpdateAdv, and BuyOffer subset, which covers the dependency
patterns central to SHACL+$\Delta$: asset creation, ownership checks,
advertisement status, and offer linkage.

\subsection{Workloads and Settings}
\label{sec:workloads}
\textbf{Datasets.} There is no public marketplace trace that carries the
constraints under test; public token traces do not encode ``open
advertisement'' or ``at most one return.'' We therefore use a synthetic
generator that already exists on both backends. Each treatment commits on
the order of $220$ transactions (Table~\ref{tab:n}); across Direct, SHACL,
and SHACL+$\Delta$, SmartChainDB commits ${\approx}670$ marketplace
transactions and Fabric repeats the same mixes (${\approx}600$ invokes).

\textbf{Mixes.} \emph{Shared ad:} one \textsc{Open} advertisement and
$n{=}20$ BuyOffers against it (identical referenced facts, distinct
tx-ids). \emph{Mutating ad:} $n/2$ BuyOffers, then UpdateAdv to
\textsc{Closed}, then $n/2$ further BuyOffers, so that the second half must
be rejected. \emph{Disjoint:} $n{=}20$ independent
Create$+$Advertise$+$BuyOffer chains as a control with no shared state.

\textbf{Probes.} A \emph{stolen-advertisement} probe submits an Advertise
whose advertiser is not among the Create owners. A \emph{conflict} probe
submits $12$ concurrent BuyOffers while an UpdateAdv to \textsc{Closed}
races them, exercising the conflict tracker.

\textbf{Validator microbenchmark.} To isolate validation cost from
consensus, one pending BuyOffer against a live \textsc{Open} advertisement
is validated $n{=}20$ times in-process under each treatment, after which
the advertisement is updated and the same offer is validated once more.
These checks do not commit additional ledger transactions.

\textbf{Load.} Closed-loop issuers post $N{=}16$ BuyOffers against one
\textsc{Open} advertisement at concurrency $C\in\{1,2,4,8\}$; offered and
achieved rates are $N$ divided by wall-clock time.

\textbf{Clusters.} One validator for latency and load; four validators for
fail-closed. Block parameters are held constant across treatments.

\textbf{Reported metrics.} Validator latency (median, p95); committed
latency (median, p95, min, max) and CDFs; cache hits, misses, and
invalidations; reject rate; offered versus achieved throughput; and
container CPU from \texttt{docker stats}.

\subsection{Experiment 1: Validation Cost and Differential Re-validation}
\label{sec:exp1}

\subsubsection{Methodology}
We measure the validator alone, starting the clock when RDF conversion or
SHACL evaluation begins and stopping it when the verdict is returned, so
that Tendermint and Fabric commit paths are excluded. We then run the three
contention mixes on the live ledger and record the SHACL+$\Delta$ cache
counters after each mix.

\subsubsection{Results: Validation Latency}
Fig.~\ref{fig:val} plots validator latency for the four conditions on a
logarithmic axis, and Table~\ref{tab:val} reports the summary statistics.
On SmartChainDB, Direct RDF conversion takes $0.23$\,ms (median; p95
$0.49$\,ms), a SHACL miss takes $13.3$\,ms (median; p95 $57.1$\,ms), and a
SHACL+$\Delta$ hit takes $0.007$\,ms. After the referenced advertisement is
updated, the next validation of the same pending offer misses and takes
$12.3$\,ms. Fabric exhibits the same ordering at a higher base cost: a miss
takes $26$\,ms, a hit under $1$\,ms, and a post-update miss $25$\,ms.

\textbf{Overhead patterns.} The SHACL miss cost is dominated by fixed
components (HTTP invocation, shape loading, and graph evaluation) rather
than by the size of the transaction, which is why the median is stable at
${\approx}13$\,ms while the tail reflects cold-engine effects on the first
requests. The SHACL+$\Delta$ hit is a hash lookup on the dependency-state
key and does not invoke the engine, which accounts for the three
orders-of-magnitude gap between hit and miss. The post-update miss is the
behavior the design requires: because the cache key is computed over the
\emph{values} of the referenced entities, an UpdateAdv changes the key, and
the stale verdict cannot be reused. Fabric's higher miss cost reflects an
additional process boundary on the endorse path rather than a different
rule set.

\subsubsection{Results: Cache Events Across Mixes}
Fig.~\ref{fig:hits} reports hits, misses, and invalidations for each mix.
On SmartChainDB, the shared, mutating, and disjoint mixes record $20$, $21$,
and $36$ misses respectively and no hits. This is a consequence of the
cache key rather than of cache failure: every committed BuyOffer carries a
fresh tx-id, so a stream of distinct offers cannot hit even when they
reference identical state. Hits arise in the validator microbenchmark,
where the \emph{same} pending transaction is re-validated, which is the
pattern that CheckTx and the mempool produce in practice. The disjoint mix
records more misses than offers because each independent advertisement
contributes referenced state that is also hashed.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig_hit_miss_invalidation.pdf}
\caption{SHACL+$\Delta$ cache events by mix.}
\label{fig:hits}
\end{figure}

On Fabric, where the client issues a second SHACL call against the same
facts before endorsement, the shared and disjoint mixes record hits
($20$ and $20$), and the mutating mix records invalidations when the
advertisement is updated. The two panels therefore reflect the same
re-validation rule under different call patterns, and should not be read
as the cache succeeding on one engine and failing on the other.

\subsection{Experiment 2: End-to-End Latency and Load}
\label{sec:exp2}

\subsubsection{Methodology}
We execute the full seven-operation lifecycle on the live ledger ($n{=}5$
trials per treatment), record the commit-latency distribution of the
shared-advertisement mix, and drive closed-loop load at
$C\in\{1,2,4,8\}$. For one trial we additionally decompose each BuyOffer
into validation time and commit/network time.

\subsubsection{Results: Full Lifecycle}
Fig.~\ref{fig:e2e} and Table~\ref{tab:e2e} report the committed lifecycle.
SmartChainDB medians are $1.55$\,s (Direct), $1.79$\,s (SHACL), and
$2.48$\,s (SHACL+$\Delta$); Fabric medians are $14.5$\,s, $14.9$\,s, and
$15.3$\,s. Across approaches, the three treatments occupy the same latency
band on each engine, and the gap between engines (roughly an order of
magnitude) is attributable to Fabric's endorse--order--commit pipeline
rather than to validation. The per-operation decomposition
(Fig.~\ref{fig:ops}--\ref{fig:where}) places the SHACL slice at
${\approx}16$\,ms on a ${\approx}250$\,ms SmartChainDB commit, so
validation accounts for a few percent of end-to-end time.

\textbf{Why $\Delta$ does not shorten the lifecycle.} Each of the seven
operations is a distinct transaction with a distinct tx-id, so the cache is

${\approx}13$\,ms matcher on a consensus-dominated path, we do not
interpret the difference as a property of the mechanism. The experiment
establishes the envelope within which validation operates and confirms that
differential re-validation is a validator-level optimization rather than a
consensus-level one.

\subsection{Experiment 3: Fail-Closed Validation Under State Change}
\label{sec:exp3}

\subsubsection{Methodology}
We run the mutating-advertisement mix ($n{=}20$) and the
stolen-advertisement probe under all three treatments on the four-validator
deployment, recording the outcome and latency of every submission. We also
run the conflict probe on SmartChainDB.

\subsubsection{Results: Rejection Behavior}
An earlier configuration of the SHACL engine accepted the stolen
advertisement and committed offers submitted after UpdateAdv to
\textsc{Closed}. The cause was that the shape consulted only the original
Advertise document, whose metadata remains \textsc{Open}, and did not
require the advertiser to be among the Create owners; Direct already
rejected the stolen advertisement, while both engines ignored subsequent
UpdateAdv status. We therefore extended the state merge so that the
\emph{effective} advertisement status is resolved from the latest UpdateAdv
and the advertiser is checked against the Create outputs, and we included
the effective status in the $\Delta$ dependency hash.

Table~\ref{tab:abort} and Fig.~\ref{fig:abort} report the re-measured
outcomes. All three SmartChainDB treatments reject the stolen advertisement
with HTTP~400 and reject all ten post-\textsc{Closed} offers, yielding a
$50\%$ reject rate on the mutating mix that matches the Fabric port. A
rejected offer returns in $16$\,ms under Direct and $33$--$36$\,ms under
SHACL and SHACL+$\Delta$; a committed offer before UpdateAdv takes
$0.76$--$0.94$\,s on the four-validator cluster.

\textbf{Interpretation.} The $50\%$ figure is the designed composition of
the mix (half the offers precede the close and half follow it) rather than
an artifact of load, and identical outcomes across Direct, SHACL, and
SHACL+$\Delta$ indicate that the declarative path is now authoritative for
the two constraints that previously leaked. Including effective status in
the dependency hash is what allows caching and correctness to coexist: an
UpdateAdv alters the key, so a verdict cached before the close cannot be
reused after it. Reject and commit latencies are different clocks---a
rejection is an HTTP~400 returned before consensus, whereas a commit
traverses four-validator Tendermint---and should not be compared as
evidence that validation is faster when it rejects. The conflict probe
records $0/12$ aborts because the ${\approx}13$\,ms validation window is
short relative to block time, so the racing UpdateAdv rarely lands inside
it; sequential fail-closed behavior does not depend on that race.

\subsubsection{Results: Four-Validator Commit Path}
Moving from one to four validators raises committed BuyOffer latency from
${\approx}200$\,ms to $0.76$--$0.94$\,s, while rejection latency is
unchanged at $16$--$36$\,ms. The increase is attributable to gossip,
voting, and four ABCI application paths rather than to validation, and it
is identical in kind across treatments. The deployment confirms that the
marketplace commits under multi-validator consensus with the same
fail-closed outcomes; it does not, and by construction cannot, increase
SHACL+$\Delta$ hit rate, since the cache is per process.

\subsection{Summary of Findings}
\label{sec:summary}
With respect to RQ1, declarative validation costs ${\approx}13$\,ms per
SHACL evaluation on SmartChainDB (${\approx}26$\,ms on Fabric), and
differential re-validation reduces that to a sub-$10\,\mu$s lookup while the
referenced state is unchanged, reverting to a full evaluation when it
changes. With respect to RQ2, the three treatments occupy the same
end-to-end latency class on each engine, validation accounts for a few
percent of committed time, and throughput is bounded by the submission
model (\texttt{broadcast\_tx\_commit}) or by endorsement overlap rather than
by validation. With respect to RQ3, after resolving effective advertisement
status and ownership in the state merge, SHACL and SHACL+$\Delta$ reject
non-owner advertisements and post-close offers with the same outcomes as
Direct, and caching preserves those outcomes because the dependency hash
includes the mutated state. With respect to RQ4, the same shapes and
treatments produce the same qualitative behavior on Fabric, with hit
patterns that differ only in the client's re-validation call pattern.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}
\textbf{Deployment scope.} Latency and load were measured on one
workstation with a single SmartChainDB validator and a two-peer Fabric
test-network; the four-validator deployment is a LAN cluster rather than a
geo-distributed one. An attempt to emulate WAN delay with
\texttt{tc netem} was denied \texttt{NET\_ADMIN} in the container runtime,
so no WAN results are reported.

\textbf{Cache scope.} SHACL+$\Delta$ state is in-process. With more than
one gunicorn worker the cache is partitioned; we pin one worker. Additional
validators exercise the commit path and do not increase hit rate.

\textbf{Construct validity.} Hit rate measured over a stream of distinct
BuyOffers is not an appropriate construct for differential re-validation,
since each offer has a fresh tx-id; the validator microbenchmark, which
re-validates one pending transaction, is the appropriate one. Rejection
latency and commit latency are measured on different paths and are not
directly comparable.

\textbf{Evaluation scope.} The workload is synthetic by necessity: public
ledgers do not record the semantic dependencies the shapes name. Each
treatment commits on the order of $220$ transactions, which is sized to
exercise a known dependency graph under repeatable conditions rather than
to characterize capacity at scale. The $0/12$ conflict-probe result bounds
in-flight abort only for the ${\approx}13$\,ms matcher measured here. HTTP
500 and ``already exists'' retries may inflate tail latency.

\textbf{Cross-platform comparison.} The Fabric port provides a portability
reference for the same shapes and treatments. We configure both systems
with identical transaction semantics, but interpret cross-platform latency
and throughput cautiously because the systems differ in runtime, storage
architecture, and consensus model; we do not report either engine as
outperforming the other.
