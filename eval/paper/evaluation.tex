\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Evaluation Objectives and Scope}
\label{sec:objectives}
Our evaluation addresses whether declarative specifications of marketplace
validation semantics can be enforced on the commit path of a permissioned
ledger at a cost that is small relative to consensus, while remaining
authoritative when the blockchain state those specifications depend upon
changes. We specifically evaluate trade-offs between (1) imperative
validation, where ownership, advertisement-status, and offer-linkage rules
are embedded in engine code, and (2) declarative SHACL constraints, where
the same rules are explicit, inspectable, and amenable to dependency-aware
re-validation.

\textbf{Evaluation criteria.} Given the inherent declarative overhead (RDF
materialization, HTTP invocation of the constraint engine, and graph
evaluation), we consider parity with the imperative Direct baseline within
the consensus-dominated latency envelope a success, as the value proposition
extends beyond validation speed to include: (1) inspectability and
portability of the constraints themselves, (2) dependency-aware
re-validation in which cached verdicts are reused only while the referenced
state is unchanged, and (3) fail-closed rejection of semantically invalid
transactions. We compare Direct, SHACL, and SHACL+$\Delta$ within
SmartChainDB (BigchainDB on Tendermint) to isolate the impact of the
validation paradigm from consensus differences, and we port the same shapes
and treatments to Hyperledger Fabric~2.5 to assess whether the observed
behavior is specific to one engine.

The focus of our evaluation is not overall system throughput, as ledger
throughput depends on consensus protocol, block parameters, network
topology, and storage backend, which are outside our scope. Rather, we aim
to understand the impact of the validation paradigm on per-transaction
cost, on the commit-path latency envelope, and on correctness under state
change. We therefore report within-engine comparisons and interpret the
Fabric panel as a portability reference rather than as a cross-platform
ranking.

Our evaluation addresses four research questions:

\textbf{RQ1: Declarative overhead and differential re-validation.} What is
the per-transaction validation cost of SHACL relative to Direct, and where
on the commit path does SHACL+$\Delta$ reuse a cached verdict rather than
re-executing constraint evaluation?

\textbf{RQ2: End-to-end impact.} How do the three approaches compare in
committed lifecycle latency, in the commit-latency distribution of
contended offers, in achieved throughput, and in validator CPU, what
fraction of commit time is attributable to validation, and how do these
change when validators are geographically distributed?

\textbf{RQ3: Fail-closed correctness.} Do SHACL and SHACL+$\Delta$ reject
semantically invalid transactions (advertisements by a non-owner, offers
against a closed advertisement) with the same outcomes as Direct, and does
verdict caching preserve those outcomes when the referenced state changes?

\textbf{RQ4: Portability.} Do the same shapes and treatments exhibit the
same qualitative behavior on Hyperledger Fabric, whose execute--order--validate
pipeline differs from Tendermint's order--execute model?

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsubsection{Hardware and Environment}
All experiments run on DigitalOcean virtual machines with 4~vCPU and
8\,GB of memory (\texttt{s-4vcpu-8gb-amd}) running Ubuntu~24.04.
SmartChainDB is deployed as four validators, each hosting Tendermint
v0.31.5, MongoDB~3.6, BigchainDB with one gunicorn worker, and the SHACL
constraint engine as a sidecar; all four hold equal voting power. In the
\emph{LAN} deployment the four validators share one private network in
New York (nyc3). In the \emph{WAN} deployment the validators are placed in
New York, Toronto, San Francisco, and Amsterdam; the Toronto region offers
only the Intel variant of the same size. Table~\ref{tab:rtt} reports
measured round-trip times. In both deployments the workload driver runs on
a separate 2~vCPU/4\,GB machine in New York and submits to the New York
validator over the private network (RTT $0.85$\,ms). The Fabric deployment
is the 2.5.12 test-network (two endorsing peers, one per organization, a
single Raft orderer, Go marketplace chaincode) on one 4~vCPU/8\,GB machine
in New York. Table~\ref{tab:setup} summarizes the configurations.

\subsubsection{Implementations}
We evaluate three implementations of identical marketplace transaction
semantics on each engine.

\textbf{Direct:} Imperative validation integrated in the engine
(server-side Python methods in SmartChainDB; Go chaincode with MVCC
version checks on Fabric). Validation logic is implemented procedurally
within the platform rather than as declarative constraints. This serves as
the baseline against which declarative overhead is measured.

\textbf{SHACL:} Declarative validation without optimization. Each
transaction is converted to RDF, merged with the referenced blockchain state
(asset ownership, effective advertisement status, offer linkage), and
validated against the shape repository over HTTP. Validation results are not
cached; every validation phase re-executes constraint evaluation. The engine
is fail-closed: a constraint violation or an unreachable engine rejects the
transaction.

\textbf{SHACL+$\Delta$:} SHACL validation with differential re-validation.
Verdicts are cached under the key $\langle$tx-id, dependency-state
hash$\rangle$, where the hash is computed over the \emph{values} of the
referenced entities (current owner, effective advertisement status, offer
state). A cached verdict is reused only if the recomputed hash matches;
committed transactions invalidate reverse-indexed entries; and a conflict
tracker may abort an in-flight validation if a dependency generation
advances during the HTTP round-trip.

On SmartChainDB a transaction is validated three times on the receiving
validator: at HTTP admission, in CheckTx when it enters the mempool, and in
DeliverTx when its block executes. Admission runs in the HTTP process,
whereas CheckTx and DeliverTx run in the ABCI consensus process, so each
validator holds two independent SHACL+$\Delta$ caches; we export the
counters of both. All three treatments share the same client, the same
transaction types, and the same state transitions on a given engine,
differing only in how validation is specified and executed. On Fabric,
SHACL and SHACL+$\Delta$ are evaluated in the client before endorsement,
and the $\Delta$ variant issues a second SHACL call against the same facts
to emulate the re-validation that Tendermint performs natively.

\subsubsection{Workload Design}
\textbf{Goals.} (1) Exercise the end-to-end dependency structure among
transaction types (owner $\to$ advertisement $\to$ offer $\to$ sale $\to$
return); (2) control the position at which referenced state changes so that
cache reuse, invalidation, and rejection can be attributed to specific
operations; (3) support both validator-only timing and committed
end-to-end timing; (4) support closed-loop load at several concurrency
levels.

\textbf{Transaction universe.} The framework supports seven marketplace
transaction types (Create, Advertise, UpdateAdv, BuyOffer, Sell,
RequestReturn, AcceptReturn). The full lifecycle experiment exercises all
seven; the contention mixes and the load experiment use the Create,
Advertise, UpdateAdv, and BuyOffer subset, which covers the dependency
patterns central to SHACL+$\Delta$: asset creation, ownership checks,
advertisement status, and offer linkage.

\subsection{Workloads and Settings}
\label{sec:workloads}
\textbf{Datasets.} There is no public marketplace trace that carries the
constraints under test; public token traces do not encode ``open
advertisement'' or ``at most one return.'' We therefore use a synthetic
generator that already exists on both backends. Each treatment commits on
the order of $220$ transactions per deployment (Table~\ref{tab:n}); across
three treatments and two deployments, SmartChainDB commits
${\approx}1{,}340$ marketplace transactions, and Fabric repeats the same
mixes.

\textbf{Mixes.} \emph{Shared ad:} one \textsc{Open} advertisement and
$n{=}20$ BuyOffers against it (identical referenced facts, distinct
tx-ids); under SHACL+$\Delta$ the driver pre-validates each offer twice
through the validation endpoint before submitting it, emulating a client
pre-check and a mempool recheck. \emph{Mutating ad:} $n/2$ BuyOffers, then
UpdateAdv to \textsc{Closed}, then $n/2$ further BuyOffers, so that the
second half must be rejected. \emph{Disjoint:} $n{=}20$ independent
Create$+$Advertise$+$BuyOffer chains as a control with no shared state.

\textbf{Probes.} A \emph{stolen-advertisement} probe submits an Advertise
whose advertiser is not among the Create owners. A \emph{conflict} probe
submits $12$ concurrent BuyOffers while an UpdateAdv to \textsc{Closed}
races them, exercising the conflict tracker.

\textbf{Validator microbenchmark.} To isolate validation cost from
consensus, one pending BuyOffer against a live \textsc{Open} advertisement
is validated $n{=}20$ times in-process on the New York validator under each
condition, after which the advertisement is updated and the same offer is
validated once more. These checks do not commit additional ledger
transactions.

\textbf{Load.} Closed-loop issuers post $N{=}16$ BuyOffers against one
\textsc{Open} advertisement at concurrency $C\in\{1,2,4,8\}$; achieved
rate is committed transactions divided by wall-clock time.

\textbf{Consensus parameters.} Tendermint runs with
\texttt{timeout\_commit}$=1$\,s, \texttt{timeout\_propose}$=3$\,s, and empty
blocks disabled; the driver submits through \texttt{broadcast\_tx\_commit}.
These are held constant across treatments and deployments.

\textbf{Reported metrics.} Validator latency (median, p95); committed
latency (median, p95, min, max) and CDFs; cache hits, misses, and
invalidations per process; reject rate; achieved throughput; and container
CPU on every validator from \texttt{docker stats} (one sample every
${\approx}3$\,s).

\subsection{Experiment 1: Validation Cost and Differential Re-validation}
\label{sec:exp1}

\subsubsection{Methodology}
We measure the validator alone, starting the clock when RDF conversion or
SHACL evaluation begins and stopping it when the verdict is returned, so
that Tendermint and Fabric commit paths are excluded. We then run the three
contention mixes on the live ledger and record the SHACL+$\Delta$ cache
counters of the HTTP and ABCI processes on the receiving validator after
each mix.

\subsubsection{Results: Validation Latency}
Fig.~\ref{fig:val} plots validator latency for the four conditions on a
logarithmic axis, and Table~\ref{tab:val} reports the summary statistics.
On SmartChainDB, the Direct check takes $0.037$\,ms (median; p95
$0.072$\,ms), a SHACL miss takes $20.9$\,ms (median; p95 $25.0$\,ms), and a
SHACL+$\Delta$ hit takes $0.009$\,ms. After the referenced advertisement is
updated, the next validation of the same pending offer misses and takes
$22.5$\,ms. Repeating the microbenchmark during the WAN campaign gives
$0.065$, $24.9$, $0.011$, and $24.1$\,ms respectively. Measured end-to-end
through the validation endpoint, the first check of a pending offer takes
$37$\,ms and the second $15$\,ms, so a hit removes ${\approx}22$\,ms and
the remainder is HTTP and JSON handling. Fabric exhibits the same ordering:
a miss takes $32$\,ms (p95 $51$\,ms), a hit under $1$\,ms, and a
post-update miss $33$\,ms; the higher miss cost reflects the client-side
engine call rather than a different rule set.

\textbf{Overhead patterns.} The SHACL miss cost is dominated by fixed
components (HTTP invocation of the engine, shape loading, and graph
evaluation) rather than by the size of the transaction, which is why the
distribution is narrow (p95 within $20\%$ of the median). The
SHACL+$\Delta$ hit is a hash lookup on the dependency-state key and does
not invoke the engine, which accounts for the gap of more than three orders
of magnitude between hit and miss. The post-update miss is the behavior the
design requires: because the cache key is computed over the \emph{values}
of the referenced entities, an UpdateAdv changes the key, and the stale
verdict cannot be reused. The Direct check is cheaper still, because it
queries the same state without materializing RDF or crossing a process
boundary; the declarative path pays ${\approx}21$\,ms per evaluation for
constraints that are explicit and portable.

\subsubsection{Results: Cache Events Across Mixes}
Fig.~\ref{fig:hits} reports hits, misses, and invalidations for each mix
and process. In the ABCI process every committed transaction misses once
in CheckTx, hits in DeliverTx, and is invalidated when its block commits:
the shared, mutating, and disjoint mixes record $20/20/20$, $11/11/11$, and
$60/60/60$ hits, misses, and invalidations, matching the $20$ offers, the
$10$ pre-close offers plus the UpdateAdv, and the $60$ chain transactions
committed in each mix. Rejected post-close offers never reach consensus and
leave no ABCI events. In the HTTP process the shared mix records $20$
misses and $40$ hits, because the first pre-validation misses and the
second pre-validation and the submission hit; the mutating and disjoint
mixes, which have no pre-validation, record only misses ($21$ and $60$).
The counts are identical in the LAN and WAN deployments.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{fig_hit_miss_invalidation.pdf}
\caption{SHACL+$\Delta$ cache events by mix: SmartChainDB HTTP process,
SmartChainDB ABCI process, and the Fabric client.}
\label{fig:hits}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{Where reuse comes from.} The counters locate reuse precisely. A
stream of distinct BuyOffers cannot hit across offers, because each offer
has a fresh tx-id; reuse arises when the \emph{same} transaction is
validated again while its dependencies are unchanged. Tendermint's
CheckTx/DeliverTx sequence produces this pattern for every committed
transaction, so SHACL+$\Delta$ removes one of the two consensus-side
evaluations unconditionally, and client-side re-checks remove the
admission evaluation as well. Invalidation on commit keeps the cache
bounded: the ABCI cache is empty after every mix. On Fabric, where the client issues a second SHACL call against the same
facts, the second call hits for every offer that passes the first: $20$
hits in the shared mix, $10$ in the mutating mix (the ten post-close offers
fail the first call and are never re-checked), and $20$ in the disjoint
mix, where Create and Advertise are validated once and only the offers
twice.

\subsection{Experiment 2: End-to-End Latency, Load, and CPU}
\label{sec:exp2}

\subsubsection{Methodology}
We execute the full seven-operation lifecycle on the live ledger ($n{=}5$
trials per treatment), record the commit-latency distribution of the
shared-advertisement mix, drive closed-loop load at $C\in\{1,2,4,8\}$, and
sample container CPU on all four validators, in both the LAN and the WAN
deployment. We combine the microbenchmark costs with the per-process cache
counters to apportion BuyOffer commit time between validation and the rest
of the commit path.

\subsubsection{Results: Full Lifecycle}
Fig.~\ref{fig:e2e} and Table~\ref{tab:e2e} report the committed lifecycle.
On the LAN, SmartChainDB medians are $4.43$\,s (Direct), $4.65$\,s (SHACL),
and $4.27$\,s (SHACL+$\Delta$); on the WAN they are $5.10$, $5.16$, and
$4.95$\,s. On Fabric the medians are $14.31$, $14.60$, and $14.57$\,s. Fig.~\ref{fig:ops} shows that every
SmartChainDB operation commits in $0.56$--$0.74$\,s on the LAN regardless
of treatment and transaction type.

\textbf{Interpretation.} Per-operation latency is set by Tendermint's
round structure (\texttt{timeout\_commit}$=1$\,s), not by validation, which
is why all seven operation types commit within the same band. SHACL adds
$0.22$\,s to the LAN lifecycle, or ${\approx}31$\,ms per operation,
consistent with three ${\approx}21$\,ms evaluations per transaction of
which part overlaps with consensus waiting. SHACL+$\Delta$ is faster than
SHACL in both deployments ($0.38$\,s on the LAN and $0.21$\,s on the WAN),
consistent with the DeliverTx hit removing one evaluation per operation. It
is also below Direct, but its range overlaps Direct's in both deployments
and $n{=}5$, so we read this as parity with Direct rather than as a speedup.
The WAN adds $0.5$--$0.7$\,s per lifecycle, or $70$--$100$\,ms per
operation, under all three treatments. On Fabric each operation takes
${\approx}2$\,s, the orderer's batch timeout, and both SHACL treatments add
$0.26$--$0.29$\,s per lifecycle, about one $32$\,ms client-side evaluation
per operation; SHACL+$\Delta$ cannot remove that evaluation, since its
second call is an extra re-check that hits.

\subsubsection{Results: Where Commit Time Goes}
Fig.~\ref{fig:where} apportions the median shared-ad BuyOffer commit
between validation and the remainder of the commit path. We estimate
validation as the number of engine evaluations on the receiving validator
times the microbenchmark cost: three Direct checks; three SHACL misses; and
for SHACL+$\Delta$, one miss (CheckTx) and two hits (admission and
DeliverTx), as recorded by the counters in Fig.~\ref{fig:hits}; each
deployment uses the microbenchmark measured during its own campaign.
Validation then accounts for under $0.03\%$ of commit time under Direct,
$9.9\%$ (LAN) and $10.2\%$ (WAN) under SHACL, and $3.4\%$ (LAN) and $3.6\%$
(WAN) under SHACL+$\Delta$.

\textbf{Interpretation.} The estimate is an upper bound on validation's
share: the measured median gap between SHACL and Direct ($15$\,ms LAN,
$34$\,ms WAN) is smaller than the $63$\,ms of serial engine work, because
CheckTx evaluation overlaps with waiting for the next proposal. Even as an
upper bound, declarative validation is about a tenth of commit time, and differential re-validation reduces it by roughly two-thirds by
removing two of the three evaluations per transaction. The share is
similar on the WAN because the longer commit is offset by a slower engine
evaluation in that campaign ($24.9$ versus $20.9$\,ms per miss).

\subsubsection{Results: Achieved Throughput}
Fig.~\ref{fig:sat} and Table~\ref{tab:tps} report achieved throughput
against concurrency. SmartChainDB sustains $1.47$--$1.61$\,tx/s on the LAN
and $1.34$--$1.41$\,tx/s on the WAN from $C{=}1$ to $C{=}8$ under all
treatments, while median latency grows linearly in $C$ (LAN Direct:
$0.63$\,s at $C{=}1$, $4.98$\,s at $C{=}8$). Fabric scales with concurrency under all treatments, from
$0.48$--$0.49$\,tx/s at $C{=}1$ to $3.78$--$3.82$\,tx/s at $C{=}8$.

\textbf{Capacity patterns.} SmartChainDB is flat in $C$ because each
validator runs a single HTTP worker and the driver submits through
\texttt{broadcast\_tx\_commit}, which holds the worker until the
transaction commits. Requests are therefore served one at a time, and
throughput equals the reciprocal of commit latency ($1/0.63$\,s
$\approx1.6$\,tx/s on the LAN; $1/0.70$\,s $\approx1.4$\,tx/s on the WAN).
The plateau is a property of the submission path, and the treatments are
indistinguishable on it; we pin one worker because the SHACL+$\Delta$ cache
is per process. Fabric scales because no server-side worker is held per transaction and
the orderer cuts a block on its $2$\,s batch timeout, so concurrent
transactions share a block; throughput is $C$ divided by the batch
interval, and the treatments differ by at most $0.04$\,tx/s.

\subsubsection{Results: Validator CPU}

uses $4.1\%$ under SHACL, and $2.4\%$ under SHACL+$\Delta$; on the WAN the

dependency merge. On the Fabric host, each peer averages $2.5$--$2.7\%$ and the orderer

\subsection{Experiment 3: Fail-Closed Validation Under State Change}
\label{sec:exp3}

\subsubsection{Methodology}
We run the mutating-advertisement mix ($n{=}20$), the stolen-advertisement
probe, and the conflict probe under all three treatments on both
deployments, recording the outcome and latency of every submission.

\subsubsection{Results: Rejection Behavior}
Two constraints require state beyond the transaction itself. An offer's
advertisement must be \textsc{Open} at validation time, but the original
Advertise document remains \textsc{Open} after a later UpdateAdv; and an
advertiser must be among the owners recorded by the Create output. The
SHACL state merge therefore resolves the \emph{effective} advertisement
status from the latest UpdateAdv and the owner set from the Create
outputs, and the effective status is part of the $\Delta$ dependency hash.

Table~\ref{tab:abort} and Fig.~\ref{fig:abort} report the outcomes. In all
six SmartChainDB runs (three treatments, two deployments) the stolen
advertisement is rejected with HTTP~400 and all ten post-close offers are
rejected, yielding a $50\%$ reject rate on the mutating mix.
Fabric produces the same outcomes under all three treatments: Direct
rejects at endorsement, where the chaincode reports that the
advertisement is not open or that the advertiser is not the current
owner, and SHACL and SHACL+$\Delta$ reject in the client before
endorsement. Rejections return before consensus: a post-close
offer is rejected in $23$\,ms (LAN) and $17$\,ms (WAN) under Direct and in
$40$--$45$\,ms under SHACL and SHACL+$\Delta$, while pre-close offers
commit in $0.59$--$0.72$\,s.

\textbf{Interpretation.} The $50\%$ figure is the designed composition of
the mix rather than an artifact of load, and identical outcomes across
treatments indicate that the declarative path is authoritative for both
state-dependent constraints. Including effective status in the dependency
hash is what allows caching and correctness to coexist: an UpdateAdv
changes the key, so a verdict cached before the close cannot be reused
after it, as the value-change miss in Experiment~1 shows directly. A
rejection is an HTTP~400 returned at admission, whereas a commit traverses
four-validator consensus, so the two latencies measure different paths.

\subsubsection{Results: Concurrent Close}
In the conflict probe, the number of the $12$ racing offers that commit
before the close varies from run to run: $4$, $8$, and $7$ under Direct,
SHACL, and SHACL+$\Delta$ on the LAN, and $3$, $3$, and $4$ on the WAN; the
remainder are rejected. The conflict tracker detected no in-flight
dependency change in any run, so every rejection came from the ordinary
status check once the UpdateAdv had committed. The split reflects where the
UpdateAdv lands in the block sequence, which varies under Direct as much as
under SHACL; the ${\approx}21$\,ms validation window is short relative to
the ${\approx}0.6$\,s block interval, so a dependency rarely changes during
one evaluation.

\subsection{Summary of Findings}
\label{sec:summary}
With respect to RQ1, a SHACL evaluation costs ${\approx}21$\,ms on a
SmartChainDB validator, and differential re-validation reduces a repeat
evaluation to ${\approx}10\,\mu$s while the referenced state is unchanged,
reverting to a full evaluation when it changes; per-process counters show
that Tendermint's CheckTx/DeliverTx sequence yields one hit per committed
transaction. With respect to RQ2, validation accounts for about $10\%$ of
commit time under SHACL and under $4\%$ under SHACL+$\Delta$; SHACL+$\Delta$
matches Direct in lifecycle and contended-offer latency on both LAN and WAN,
reduces engine CPU by $41$--$49\%$ relative to SHACL, and throughput is
bounded by the submission path rather than by validation. Distributing
validators across four regions adds $76$--$95$\,ms per commit under every
treatment. With respect to RQ3, SHACL and SHACL+$\Delta$ reject
non-owner advertisements and post-close offers with the same outcomes as
Direct on both deployments, and caching preserves those outcomes because
the dependency hash includes the mutated state. With respect to RQ4,
the same shapes and treatments yield the same verdicts on Fabric, with a
$32$\,ms miss and a sub-millisecond hit, the same fail-closed outcomes,
and lifecycle, commit, and throughput differences between treatments
within $2\%$; there, reuse arises from the client's re-check rather than
from the CheckTx/DeliverTx sequence.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}
\textbf{Deployment scope.} SmartChainDB runs four validators, the minimum
that tolerates one Byzantine fault; we did not vary validator count. The
WAN deployment spans four regions of one provider, and the Toronto
validator uses an Intel rather than an AMD processor of the same size. The
Fabric test-network runs all peers and the orderer on one machine, so its
latencies exclude inter-peer network delay.

\textbf{Submission path.} With one HTTP worker and
\texttt{broadcast\_tx\_commit}, SmartChainDB throughput is the reciprocal of
commit latency; our throughput figures characterize this configuration,
not the engine's capacity. More workers would partition the per-process
cache.

\textbf{Cache scope.} SHACL+$\Delta$ state is per process. The HTTP and ABCI
processes hold separate caches, so a hit at admission does not avoid the
CheckTx miss. Hit rate measured over a stream of distinct BuyOffers is not
an appropriate construct for differential re-validation, since each offer
has a fresh tx-id; per-process counters and the microbenchmark are.

\textbf{Estimation.} The validation share of commit time is derived from
microbenchmark costs and event counts rather than from instrumentation of
the commit path, and is an upper bound because part of the work overlaps
with consensus waiting.

\textbf{Evaluation scope.} The workload is synthetic by necessity: public
ledgers do not record the semantic dependencies the shapes name. Each
treatment commits on the order of $220$ transactions per deployment, which
is sized to exercise a known dependency graph under repeatable conditions
rather than to characterize capacity at scale. Lifecycle results use
$n{=}5$ trials.

\textbf{Cross-platform comparison.} The Fabric port provides a portability
reference for the same shapes and treatments. We configure both systems
with identical transaction semantics, but interpret cross-platform latency
and throughput cautiously because the systems differ in runtime, storage
architecture, deployment topology, and consensus model; we do not report
either engine as outperforming the other.
